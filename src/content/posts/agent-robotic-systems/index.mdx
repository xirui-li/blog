## The Agent–Environment Loop

Every robot policy can be read as the same loop: an agent receives a [task](#task-instruction-and-prior-knowledge), [acts on its environment, and observes what happens](#interface-observation-and-action-spaces). An LLM-based agent makes this loop explicit, [reasoning about what it knows](#agent-policy-and-memory), acting, and reading the result before it reasons again, as in ReAct [1]. Figure 1 is the map for the rest of this post, from the loop's [timing](#time-steps-chunks-and-asynchrony) to [learning across episodes](#learning-meta-learning-continual-learning-and-rsi) and how [VLAs, WAMs, and LLM-based agents](#vla-vs-wam-vs-llm-based-agent) fill it in.

## Task: Instruction and Prior Knowledge

{/* Figure 1: the Task box (g, K) — set once per episode. Two subsections: what the robot is asked to do (g), and what it is given to know (K). */}

### Instruction

The most common instruction is a sentence of natural language. End-to-end policies such as RT-2 [5] take a short command like "put the cup in the basket", and an LLM-based agent receives it as a text prompt. Language is easy to give, but often ambiguous: which cup, and placed where?

Other formats trade that ease for precision (Table 1). A goal image shows the finished scene [6], a sketched trajectory shows the motion [7], and a video shows a person doing the task once [8]. Multimodal prompts mix text with images [9], visual marks point at where to act [10], and formal specifications such as PDDL goals make the task checkable by a planner [11]. The more precise the format, the more effort it takes to provide, and the more irrelevant detail it fixes.

<Figure
  kind="table"
  compact
  title="Ways to give a robot its task."
  caption="Formats range from abstract to specific: language is easy to give but ambiguous, while images and videos are precise but also fix details that do not matter."
>

| Format | Example | Specifies | Representative work |
|---|---|---|---|
| Language | "Put the cup in the basket" | Goal | RT-2 [5] |
| Goal image | A photo of the finished scene | Goal | Play-LMP [6] |
| Sketch or trajectory | A path drawn over the camera image | Goal or motion | RT-Trajectory [7] |
| Video demonstration | A person doing the task once | Process | Vid2Robot [8] |
| Multimodal prompt | "Put the [image] into the [image]" | Goal | VIMA [9] |
| Visual marks | Points or arrows drawn on the image | Where to act | PIVOT [10] |
| Formal specification | A PDDL goal or temporal-logic formula | Goal and constraints | LLM+P [11] |

</Figure>

The bigger difference is who reads the instruction. An end-to-end policy accepts only the formats it was trained on; adding sketches or videos means new data and retraining. An LLM-based agent can take anything it can read in context, such as text, images, rules, or a demonstration, and can ask a clarifying question when the instruction is ambiguous.

{/* Writing outline (from the research notes):

    - What an instruction specifies: the goal (what), the process (how), or constraints (what not to do).

## Interface: Observation and Action Spaces

{/* Figure 1: the two edges between agent and environment — the only way they interact.
    Observation space (o_{t+1}, r_{t+1}):
    - raw pixels and proprioception vs. structured perception (objects, poses) vs. skill return values ("grasp failed, cup on table");
    - active perception: "look" as an action (e.g. detect("cup")) instead of passively receiving every frame;
    - is the reward r visible at run time? Usually not — mostly for training and evaluation.
    Action space (a_t): low-level commands → skill calls → programs (code as policy).
    - What separates them: what an action means, whether it returns a result, and who decides when it ends.
    - Action chunking does NOT change the action space (still low-level commands, just k at a time) — see "Time".
    Skill design (the agent-side form of the action space):
    - interface: name, arguments, description for the model, pre/postconditions;
    - a skill's return value is an observation — this closes the loop with the observation space;
    - an end-to-end policy (VLA) can itself be one skill. */}

## Agent: Policy and Memory

{/* Figure 1: the agent box — Memory M and Policy π, joined by m_t / b_t and executed a_t.
    Memory M (internal state, not an input; outline from the four questions):
    - representation & capacity: raw history, summaries, world state, or a belief distribution?
    - writing & forgetting: what gets written (observations, executed actions, g, K), when it is compressed or dropped;
    - reading: what part of M reaches the policy each step (context construction — the m_t / b_t edge);
    - persistence: within one episode, or across tasks?
    - End-to-end: m_t ≈ the latest few frames. Agent: explicit execution record, world state, possibly long-term memory.
    Policy π:
    - end-to-end: one forward pass from observation to action; any reasoning stays inside the network;
    - agent: ReAct-style — reason about m_t, choose an action, read its result, reason again. */}

## Time: Steps, Chunks, and Asynchrony

{/* Figure 1: "t = 0, 1, 2, …" — the time structure of the agent–environment loop.
    - Commitment horizon: how long one decision is executed before looking again — single step → action chunk → skill (a skill is a nested inner loop inside a_t).
    - Sync vs async: does the policy wait while a_t executes, or keep running? (async chunked inference ↔ agents monitoring background skills)
    - Interruption & recovery: issuing a new a_t before the last one finishes.
    - Figure idea: horizon (step / chunk / skill) × sync / async, as a 2D grid.
      e.g. a chunked VLA = low-level action space + medium horizon; a synchronous agent = skill action space + long, variable horizon. */}

## VLA vs. WAM vs. LLM-Based Agent

VLAs, WAMs, and LLM-based agents are not three rungs of one ladder: a VLA decides how to move, a WAM also predicts what will happen, and an LLM-based agent decides what to do next, often by calling the other two as skills [15, 16].

{/* Framing: the three are not on the same layer. An LLM-based agent orchestrates (what to do, which capability to call); a VLA is an action model (how to move next); a WAM couples action with prediction (what happens if I act). Comparing "LLM-based agent vs VLA/WAM" often compares a whole orchestration system with one of its action models — so this is not a ranking. A VLA can use a VLM backbone, and an agent can call a VLA as one of its skills.

    Background on the end-to-end families (inputs and outputs):
    - VLA, π0.5 [2] / MolmoAct 2 [3]: images + instruction (+ proprioception) → fixed-rate action chunks. π0.5 predicts a subtask in language first; MolmoAct 2 reasons spatially before acting. Reasoning stays inside one forward pass.
    - WAM, DreamZero [4] (named the family): same input → future video + the actions that produce it, from a video-diffusion backbone running closed loop. Reported to generalize to unseen environments and objects better than strong pretrained VLAs in its setting — one setting, not a general ranking.

    Where an agent's advantage comes from (analysis, not a proven rule):
    1. Transferable knowledge — web-scale vision-language data vs scarce, embodiment-bound robot data. But VLAs also try to inherit VLM knowledge (e.g. RT-2); what matters is whether it survives training.
    2. Composing existing skills — a new task becomes a new combination of reliable skills. Bounded by the skill library: a correct plan cannot open a bottle without a bottle-opening skill.
    3. Runtime mechanisms, not the model — keeping history, checking real outcomes, replanning. These can be added around a VLA too. Explaining a failure is not the same as detecting one; checks must rest on environment feedback.
    4. Versus WAM — task-level reasoning may be enough when the question is ordering ("clear the cups or wipe the table first?"); predicting future states helps more with contact, sliding, and novel motions, at extra compute and compounding prediction error.

    What a fair comparison must control: keep the same executor and add VLM planning, memory, and outcome checks one at a time; test new instruction combinations, new objects, new physical motions, and interruptions; do not compare success rates across different real-robot setups.

    Cited: Towards Embodied Agentic AI [15]; Large VLM-based VLA Models for Robotic Manipulation: A Survey [16].
    Other surveys from the research notes (not verified, not cited): Agentic LLM-based robotic systems … agenticness and ethics (Frontiers in Robotics and AI, 2025); World Action Models: The Next Frontier in Embodied AI (May 2026); World-Action Models for Robot Learning and Control: A Survey (Sep 2026). */}

## Open Questions

1. **How do we reduce the latency of agent systems?** Each reasoning step is far slower than a control loop, so the agent has to decide [how long to commit and whether to keep thinking while it acts](#time-steps-chunks-and-asynchrony).
2. **How do agent systems handle fine-grained manipulation?** An agent is only as dexterous as the skills it can call; contact-rich tasks may need [VLA or WAM policies as skills](#vla-vs-wam-vs-llm-based-agent).
3. **What is the right harness interface for VLM agents?** What the agent observes, how skills are described, and what each call returns together define [the interface](#interface-observation-and-action-spaces) it reasons over.
4. **Could we build MCP servers for robotics?** The [Model Context Protocol](https://modelcontextprotocol.io) standardizes how LLM agents connect to tools and data; robot skills and sensors could be exposed the same way.
5. **How do we define physical intelligence?** The term is used everywhere but rarely pinned down: is it dexterity, an understanding of physics, or [the whole loop](#the-agentenvironment-loop) working well under real-world uncertainty?
